# Handling a Motor cycle (Manouvres)

## 1. Setting Your Motorcycle in Motion

This manoeuvre should be performed smoothly to ensure better control and balance.

- Initiating: Place your right foot on the footrest and your left foot on the ground while pressing your right knee against the gas tank. Apply both the front brake lever and the rear brake pedal.

- Executing: Squeeze the clutch lever and shift into first gear. Release the front brake while keeping the rear brake pressed. Gradually release the clutch to the friction point (where the bike begins to edge forward) while simultaneously turning the throttle to increase engine speed.

- Completing: Release the rear brake pedal as the bike moves, accelerate gradually, and place your left foot on the footrest


## 2. Shifting Gears

- To Accelerate: Release the throttle, squeeze the clutch, and raise the gearshift lever to the next higher gear. Release the clutch gradually while increasing the throttle. Skipping gears when accelerating can significantly increase the time needed for manoeuvres like passing.

- To Decelerate (Downshifting): Release the throttle, squeeze the clutch, and lower the gearshift lever. Do not skip gears when downshifting, as this causes heavy engine compression and can shift weight forward, potentially causing a loss of control


## 3. Braking

Braking must be done **gradually using both wheels** simultaneously. The front brake provides approximately **70%** of the braking power, while the rear brake provides **30%** and helps keep the bike stable.

- Execution: Release the throttle and squeeze the clutch lever. Apply the rear brake pedal gradually, followed by the front brake lever, ensuring neither wheel locks up


## 4. Coming to a Stop

This is used when you have enough distance to slow down gradually.

- Execution: Release the throttle, squeeze the clutch, and downshift gear by gear until you reach first gear. Gradually apply the rear and then the front brakes until you stop.

- Completing: Shift into neutral or stay in first gear with the clutch squeezed, and place your left foot on the ground


## 5. Emergency Braking (exception)

Reacting properly in an emergency is not intuitive and requires practice.

- What to do: Release the throttle to allow engine braking to help. Do not downshift; focus exclusively on braking. Apply the rear brake energetically up to the locking threshold, then use the powerful front brake without locking the wheel. Squeeze the clutch lever just before stopping so the engine doesn't continue to push the bike forward.


## 6. Emergency Braking in a Curve

If you must brake in a curve, you must account for the bike’s inclination. Start braking while leaning, but pull the bike upright as quickly as possible. Once upright, apply full braking force while looking toward your target destination.


## 7. Making a Turn (Speeds Below 25 km/h)

This is used for sharp angles, such as at intersections.

- Initiating: Look far ahead toward the exit of the turn.

- Executing: Release the throttle to slow down, turn the handlebars in the direction of the turn, and counterbalance by shifting your body weight toward the outside of the turn to maintain balance.

- Completing: Gradually turn the throttle to accelerate as the bike returns to an upright position


## 8. Negotiating a Curve (Speeds Above 25 km/h)

High-speed curves are common on highways.

- Initiating: Look far ahead toward the exit of the curve and slow down before entering.

- Executing: Use countersteering by pushing forward on the handlebar on the same side as the curve (e.g., push the right handlebar for a right-hand curve). Maintain a constant speed throughout the curve.

- Completing: As you exit, gradually shift your body back to center and accelerate to a safe speed

## 9. Avoiding an Obstacle

The technique used depends entirely on your speed.

- Scanning: Do not stare at the obstacle; look at the point where you want to steer the bike.

- Execution: Release the throttle slightly to reduce speed.

    - Below 25 km/h: Use the counterbalancing technique.
    - Above 25 km/h: Use the countersteering technique by pushing the handlebar on the side of the direction you wish to go.





# Handling a Motor cycle on the road (Manouvres)

 Based on the sources, here is a detailed explanation of the manoeuvres to be performed on the road while operating a motorcycle.

## Executing Manoeuvres on the Road

Mastering these manoeuvres is essential for preventing accidents and becoming a safe, cooperative, and responsible motorcyclist. On the road, you must apply the basic handling skills learned on a closed track while simultaneously accounting for other road users, traffic regulations, and road signs.

### Essential Reminders for Road Manoeuvres

- Safety Techniques: Always adopt a safe riding position and scan the right areas when performing any manoeuvre.

- The Three Steps: Every manoeuvre is divided into three distinct phases: initiating it, executing it, and completing it.

- Impact of Speed: High speed significantly reduces your field of vision and makes it harder to maintain an overall view of the road. At 100 km/h, your field of vision is reduced by about one-half.



# Specific Road Manoeuvres

## 1. Making a Turn at an Intersection

Visibility is a major factor in intersection accidents; you must ensure you are visible to others and that you see them.

- Initiating: Slow down, check your mirrors and blind spots, activate your turn signal, and position yourself in the correct third of the lane.

- Executing: Look toward the exit point of the turn and scan the environment from left to right while following a course that maintains your safety margins.

- Completing: Position yourself in the appropriate third of the lane, gradually resume speed, and turn off your turn signal.


## 2. Negotiating a Curve

Accidents in curves are often caused by failing to adapt speed to the radius of the curve.

- Initiating: Keep your head up, look far toward the curve's exit, and slow down before entering.

- Executing: Maintain a constant speed, continue looking at the exit, and use countersteering (pushing the handlebar on the side of the curve).

- Completing: Gradually resume speed as the motorcycle returns to an upright position.

- Successive Curves: On winding roads, avoid accelerating between curves to ensure you aren't travelling too fast for the next one.


## 3. Entering a Highway

- Initiating (On-ramp): Check mirrors and your left blind spot, and activate your left turn signal.

- Executing (Acceleration Lane): Accelerate gradually to match the speed of highway traffic while maintaining safety margins.

- Completing (Merging): Merge near the end of the dotted line, position yourself in the left third of the lane to be visible, and turn off your signal.


## 4. Exiting a Highway

- Initiating: Check mirrors and your right blind spot, and activate your right turn signal.

- Executing: Wait until you are in the deceleration lane before slowing down gradually to the prescribed speed.

- Completing: Position yourself in the left third of the lane in the off-ramp and turn off your signal.


## 5. Passing Another Vehicle

Never pass without ensuring it can be done safely, and always obey the speed limit.

- Initiating: Evaluate the speed of vehicles ahead and oncoming traffic. Check mirrors and blind spots, then activate your left turn signal.

- Executing: Accelerate firmly to pass quickly (without speeding), maintain safety margins, and signal right.

- Completing: Move back into the original lane once there is enough space, position in the left third, and turn off your signal.


## 6. Parking

Park in the middle of a space at a 45-degree angle, pointed toward traffic flow.

- Initiating: Signal your intention, check mirrors and blind spots, and slow down.

- Executing: Shift to neutral and back the bike up until the rear tire touches or is very close to the curb.

- Completing: Turn off the engine and flashers, and prop the bike on its kickstand.


## 7. Prohibited Manoeuvres

For safety, the following actions are strictly prohibited:

- Lane splitting (driving between two rows of vehicles in adjacent lanes).

- Driving between a moving vehicle and the shoulder.

- Driving between a row of moving vehicles and a row of parked vehicles


# Choosing the Correct Third of Your Lane to Ride in

When operating a motorcycle, you should imagine your traffic lane as being divided into three equal strips (left, middle, and right thirds). Because a motorcycle is smaller than an automobile, choosing the correct position within these thirds is a vital driving technique for preventing risks.

There is no single "best" position for all situations; the correct third is the one that provides you with maximum visibility and adequate safety margins based on current driving and weather conditions.

Characteristics of the Three Positions:

## 1. The Left Third

This is generally the most frequently used position.

- Best for: Passing other vehicles or making a left turn.

- Visibility: Provides a better view of vehicles ahead and oncoming traffic while making you more visible to others.

- Safety: Puts more distance between you and parked cars and usually prevents other vehicles from trying to squeeze into your lane while passing you.

- Caution: Ruts from heavy traffic often form here, which can collect rainwater and cause a loss of control.


## 2. The Middle Third

- Best for: Travelling in the middle lane of a three-lane highway to maintain margins from vehicles on both sides.

- Windy Conditions: This is a strategic position in high winds, as it provides more room to recover if a strong gust blows you off course.

- Caution: Motor oil tends to accumulate in the center of the lane; it can become extremely slippery when it rains.


## 3. The Right Third

- Best for: Making a right turn.

- Heavy Vehicles: Protects you from the air turbulence created by large oncoming or passing trucks.

- Hills: This is the safest position when arriving at the top of a hill where you cannot see oncoming traffic.

- Caution: Like the left third, this area is prone to ruts that can collect water.


## General Safety Tips for Lane Positioning

- Maximize Visibility: Always choose the position where you are most visible to others and have the best view of the road ahead.

- Avoid "Lane Sharing": As much as possible, position yourself to prevent motorists or other motorcyclists from travelling alongside you in the same lane.

- Group Riding: When riding in a group of two or more, you are legally required to use a zigzag formation. In this setup, odd-numbered riders travel in the left third and even-numbered riders travel in the right third, each maintaining a two-second safety margin


# Law of Physics (GGICKT)

Operating a motorcycle is subject to the laws of physics, regardless of the rider's experience level. These physical principles subject the driver to forces that directly affect balance, control, and overall safety.

## 1. Gravity

Gravity is the force of attraction the Earth exerts on all physical bodies. In motorcycling, this law explains why a vehicle slows down when traveling uphill and accelerates when going down. Gravity significantly impacts balance; for instance, adding extra weight like a passenger or baggage can shift the motorcycle's **center of gravity, which is the point that prevents the bike from falling to one side or the other**.

## 2. Gyroscopic Effect (maintains a wheel while turning)

The gyroscopic effect is the **force that maintains a wheel on its axis while it is turning**. This effect explains why a motorcycle can **remain in balance by itself once it is set in motion**. The faster the wheels rotate, the stronger and more stable this effect becomes. Conversely, **at low speeds, the gyroscopic effect is almost nonexistent**, making it much harder to maintain balance.
 
## 3. Inertia (straight line, needs force to turn)

Inertia is the physical law stating that **a body in motion will continue to move in a straight line unless another force causes it to slow down or change trajectory**. Because of inertia, a motorcycle moving forward naturally tends to continue in that direction. To change directions, a rider must use **specific techniques—such as countersteering—to compensate** for this tendency to stay on a straight course.

## 4. Centrifugal Force (outward force)

**Centrifugal force pulls a vehicle outward when it is traveling through a turn or curve at speeds typically above 25 km/h**. This force increases in proportion to the motorcycle’s weight, its speed, and the sharpness (radius) of the curve. **To offset this outward pull, a rider must lean the motorcycle toward the inside of the turn.**
Countersteering, to offset centrifugal force.


## 5. Kinetic Energy and Force of Impact

Kinetic energy is the **energy accumulated by a body in motion**, and it is **determined by the vehicle's weight and speed**. For example, a motorcycle traveling at 90 km/h stores four times more kinetic energy than one traveling at 45 km/h.

- Dissipation: This energy must be evacuated, either through heat (brakes), friction (tires on the road), or, in a collision, the deformation of metal.

- Force of Impact: In a crash, this energy is absorbed by the vehicle, the obstacle, and the motorcyclist's body, often with enough force to twist metal.


# Traction (Coefficient of Friction)

Traction is the **resistance between the road surface** and the tires, which are a motorcycle's only points of contact with the ground. It is measured by the coefficient of friction, which depends on the condition of both the road (e.g., dry, wet, or icy) and the tire (e.g., wear and pressure).

- Safety Margin: The higher the coefficient, the greater the traction.

- Braking Distance: Worn tires can drastically increase a motorcycle's braking distance. Traction is highest on dry surfaces and lowest on surfaces like gravel, wet pavement, or ice.
